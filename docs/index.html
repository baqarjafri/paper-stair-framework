STAIR: A Five-Level Framework for Evaluating LMS Readiness for Agentic AI Teaching Agents

Baqar Jafri

University of Stirling, Computing Science and Mathematics · ORCID 0009-0007-3204-0460 · baqar@nexgenclass.com

Abstract

Abstract.Autonomous AI teaching agents promise personalised instruction at scale, but the learning management systems that would host them were designed for human workflows, not agentic ones. This paper shows the mismatch is not a matter of capability but of architecture. Applying a new five-level readiness framework, STAIR (Scaffold for Teaching Agent Integration Readiness), to Moodle 5.2dev+ reveals a 28-percentage-point cliff between student-state infrastructure (53%) and agent-reasoning infrastructure (25%). The cause is code-level isolation between the platform's AI and analytics subsystems. STAIR derives its levels from converging requirements in Zimmerman's self-regulated learning cycle, Talebirad and Nadiri's multi-agent collaboration architecture, and a recent educational LLM agent taxonomy. The framework operationalises these into 28 sub-dimensions, each scored against an evidence-linked rubric through systematic source-code analysis. Moodle scores 55% at event sensing (L1) and 53% at student state (L2) but falls to 25% at agent reasoning (L3), 5% at orchestration (L4), and 22% at intervention delivery (L5). The platform provides no native support for autonomous agent coordination or pedagogically timed response. Until learning management systems bridge language-model reasoning and learner data, the field's growing investment in educational AI remains structurally stranded. The ceiling is set by the architecture of the host platforms, not by the capability of the models.

Keywords: STAIR framework · learning management systems · AI teaching agents · self-regulated learning · agent architecture · Moodle · platform readiness

CORE FINDINGS

What the paper found

Four findings carry the paper. Each chart is built from the repository's data files; hover and select to inspect the numbers behind it.

Finding 1 · the readiness profile

Readiness does not decline gradually. It drops 28 points between the data levels and the reasoning level.

Moodle 5.2dev+ scores 55% at event sensing and 53% at student state, then 25% at agent reasoning, 5% at orchestration and 22% at intervention delivery. The fall between L2 and L3 is a discontinuity, not a slope.

Finding 2 · where it breaks

Two of L3's five dimensions score zero: no reasoning pipeline, no AI-to-analytics bridge.

Of 28 checks, nine are absent from the Moodle source. Three of them sit at L3 (agent identity model, reasoning pipeline, AI-analytics bridge) and pull the level to 25%. L4 has four absences; L5 has none of the timing intelligence an intervention needs.

What was checked

Whether code-level connections exist between the AI subsystem and the analytics or learner-state framework, so that an agent can reason over learner state without each deployment writing a bespoke adapter. This is the paper's central empirical finding: Moodle scores 0 here because the two subsystems share zero imports.

Finding 3 · the mechanism

The AI subsystem and the analytics framework share zero code-level imports.

Moodle can generate a language-model response, and Moodle can predict which students are at risk, but no architectural pathway connects the two. Each AI request is a stateless request-response pair with no access to learner context. The gap is bridgeable through the plugin system without touching core: roughly 150 to 200 lines, an estimate from source analysis that remains to be validated.

Finding 4 · the instrument

Five levels, in a strict dependency chain, grounded in three literatures that converge on the same structure.

STAIR (Scaffold for Teaching Agent Integration Readiness) turns "supports AI" into five architectural conditions an autonomous teaching agent needs from its host: sense, remember, reason, coordinate, reach. Each level is where Zimmerman's self-regulated learning cycle, Talebirad and Nadiri's multi-agent architecture, and Chu et al.'s educational LLM-agent taxonomy meet. Any LMS can be scored against it.

Requirement: Platform delivers context-aware, timely agent interventions embedded within the flow of learning activity.

Zimmerman: Adaptive self-reactionsTalebirad and Nadiri: Delivery mechanismChu et al.: Personalisation and real-time adaptation

Requirement: Platform coordinates concurrent AI agents sharing student context, with conflict resolution and load management.

Zimmerman: Metacognitive monitoringTalebirad and Nadiri: Multi-agent coordinationChu et al.: Multi-agent communication

Requirement: Platform supports autonomous AI deliberation over student state, with platform-enforced pedagogical guardrails.

Zimmerman: Strategic planningTalebirad and Nadiri: Agent reasoning loopChu et al.: Planning and goal decomposition

Requirement: Platform maintains a persistent, queryable model of each learner's cumulative progress, performance, and risk status.

Zimmerman: Self-judgmentTalebirad and Nadiri: Knowledge representationChu et al.: Memory (short and long-term)

Requirement: Platform emits structured, pedagogically classified learner events accessible to AI consumers.

Zimmerman: Self-observationTalebirad and Nadiri: Event sensing layerChu et al.: Contextual input

Each level depends on the one below it: a platform that fails at L1 cannot support L2, because agents have no perceptual input.

How it was measured

Design science research: the instrument was derived from theory, then applied to Moodle's open source tree by structured source-code analysis. Every score points at a file.

STEP 128 checks, scored 0 to 4Absent, nascent, partial, substantial, ready. A zero requires a source search that proves absence.
STEP 2Evidence hierarchySource code outranks API documentation, which outranks runtime behaviour, which outranks documentation.
STEP 3Five dimensions per levelEqual-weight consolidation into coverage, accessibility, educational specificity, temporal availability, integration.
STEP 4Level readinessMean of the five dimensions: 55 / 53 / 25 / 5 / 22. Provenance and weights published.

Limitations

  • Dimensional structure derived from theoretical convergence, not empirical factor analysis; other weightings could shift level scores.
  • Single rater, no inter-rater reliability yet; the instrument is built for replication.
  • One platform at one point in time; patterns may not transfer to other technology stacks.
  • Source code, not runtime: STAIR measures structural readiness, not operational readiness.

Next steps

  • Multi-platform evaluation: Open edX and Canvas LMS first, to test whether the cliff generalises and to produce inter-rater data.
  • Longitudinal re-evaluation of Moodle as its AI subsystem matures.
  • Sensitivity analysis of the uniform dimension weights once multi-platform data exist.
